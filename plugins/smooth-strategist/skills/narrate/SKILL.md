# Narrate

Arrange what was found into a story a busy reader can follow and act on: the answer first, the reasons under it, the evidence under each reason.

Third of five skills in one cycle: `frame` → `analyze` → **narrate** → `decide` → `plan`. Each works alone. If the user comes straight here, state in one sentence what the central question is and which findings carry the story, and go on.

The structure is the same for a board paper, an engineering proposal, a research summary or a note to a landlord. Tone and vocabulary come from the audience.

Respond in the user's language.

## Flow

1. State the central question and who the reader is, and decide what is being asked for: **the piece itself** (an executive summary, an email, a memo, a note, the story of what happened) or **a storyline** for something longer that someone will build afterwards (a deck, a long document).
2. Redo the arithmetic on the numbers the user brought, before building anything on them.
3. Pick the structure (table below).
4. Write the governing idea and the message of each section. When the piece itself was asked for, then write the piece.
5. Check the story.

## Which structure to use

| Situation | Use |
| --- | --- |
| A recommendation, in a document or deck, for someone who decides | **Pyramid** |
| The opening of any of these: the first slide, an executive summary, an email that recommends something | **SCQA**, on top of a pyramid |
| Telling what already happened: a retrospective, a case, a post-mortem | **Story Spine** |

## Pyramid

From Barbara Minto, *The Pyramid Principle*. The answer comes first.

Three levels:

1. **Governing idea**: the answer, in one sentence.
2. **Arguments**: the main reasons behind the answer. Usually three; between two and five.
3. **Evidence**: the data, analysis or logic behind each argument.

How arguments relate to each other (horizontal logic), one of two ways:

- **Parts of a whole**: each argument supports the governing idea on its own. Removing one weakens the case but does not break it.
- **Chain**: each argument builds on the one before until the conclusion follows. Removing one breaks the case, so use a chain only when every link is solid.

How levels relate (vertical logic): each level answers the question the level above raises in the reader's mind, either "why?" or "how?".

The arguments should not overlap, and together they should be enough to support the governing idea.

Example:

- Governing idea: route tickets by product before hiring anyone.
- Arguments: the delay is in the queue, not in the work; the queue grew because one form feeds one queue; routing is a two-week change and hiring takes a quarter.
- Evidence under the first: time being worked is flat at 40 minutes; time waiting went from 6 to 19 hours.

## SCQA: Situation, Complication, Question, Answer

Also from Minto. The short form of the same idea, for openings.

1. **Situation**: the context the reader already accepts.
2. **Complication**: what changed and calls for action.
3. **Question**: the question that follows.
4. **Answer**: the governing idea.

Then the arguments in pyramid form, and the next steps.

By default lead with the answer and give situation and complication after it, in a sentence each. Keep the S-C-Q-A order when the reader lacks the context or is likely to resist the answer.

## Story Spine

Adapted from Kenn Adams. The sequence, with "because of that" repeated as many times as the story needs:

> Once upon a time ___. Every day ___. But one day ___. Because of that ___. Because of that ___. Until finally ___. And ever since then ___.

- **Beginning**: the context and the routine before anything changed.
- **Event**: what happened that broke the routine.
- **Middle**: the chain of consequences and actions, each caused by the one before.
- **End**: where it landed and what is different now.

Drop the fairy-tale wording in the final text; keep the causal chain. This works for telling what already happened. For a forward-looking recommendation use the pyramid, and show the end state early.

## Checking the story

- The central idea fits in one sentence.
- Each section or slide carries one message.
- Read in order, the section messages alone tell the story.
- Every claim has evidence from the analysis.
- The story survives the user's own numbers. If a number weakens the proposal (the alternative costs more than the current setup, the target does not close on their own figures), it goes into the story at the point where the audience would ask, already with the right reading. The audience will find the number anyway, and it is better coming from whoever presents. Do not leave it in a note outside the document.
- The text states only what the user gave. A cause or a detail that was inferred is either left out or raised with the user in the note, not written into the piece as fact.

## What to deliver

The user wants the story, not an explanation of the structure. Name the structure at most once, if that helps the reader. The checks do not become sections.

**When the piece itself was asked for, write it**, complete and ready to send, in the voice of whoever will sign it and addressed to its reader. "Write the exec summary" or "tell the story of the project" asks for the text, not for an outline of it; an outline with notes on what to say in each part leaves the user with the writing still to do. Keep it to the length the format implies: an executive summary fits on a page.

Anything meant for the user rather than for the reader stays out of the piece: a correction to their brief, a claim you did not make because their numbers do not support it, an assumption, a gap, an option for a stronger version. Put those in a short note before or after the piece, clearly separated from it, so the piece can be copied and sent as it is.

**When a storyline was asked for** (a deck, a long document someone will build), deliver the central idea in one sentence and the sequence of sections or slides, each with its heading and what goes in it.

Follow the user's heading style: some want the message as the heading, others a neutral label with the message in the body.

Facts that change the story go inside the story, not in the note: the reader has to see them. Assumptions and missing data go in the note to the user, in a few lines. Cut what does not change what the user will present.

Prose, lists and tables follow the user's format and tone preferences when they have any; plain prose with few headings is the default for a piece, and short structured text for a storyline. If the user asks for slides or a formatted file, use the skills the environment has, with this storyline as content.
