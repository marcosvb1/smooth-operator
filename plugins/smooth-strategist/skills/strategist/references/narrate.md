# Narrate

Turn findings into something a busy reader can follow and act on. A capable model already writes clearly; this stage holds the few rules that keep the piece honest and ready to use.

Third of five stages: `frame` → `analyze` → **narrate** → `decide` → `plan`.

Respond in the user's language.

## What to do

1. Decide what is being asked for: **the piece itself** (an executive summary, an email, a memo, a note, the story of what happened) or **a storyline** for something longer that someone will build afterwards (a deck, a long document).
2. Redo the arithmetic on the numbers the user brought, before building anything on them.
3. Put the answer first, the reasons under it and the evidence under each reason (Barbara Minto, *The Pyramid Principle*). Two to five reasons that do not overlap. For an opening, one sentence each of situation and complication after the answer is enough; start with them only when the reader lacks the context or is likely to resist.
4. For the story of something that already happened, keep the causal chain: how things were, what broke the routine, what followed from what, where it landed (after Kenn Adams's Story Spine).
5. Write it, then check it against the rules below.

## Rules

**Do not claim what the numbers do not support.** If the user wants to say "this saves money from day one" or "with this we hit the target" and their own figures say otherwise, write what the figures do support and tell the user why, in the note. The reader will do the arithmetic anyway, and it is better coming from whoever signs.

**The unfavourable fact goes inside.** A cost that is higher in the first year, a deadline that slipped, a budget that was exceeded: state it in the piece, at the point where the reader would ask, already with the right reading. Left out, it becomes the thing the reader remembers.

**Only what the user gave.** No invented causes, dates, amounts, reactions or results, however natural they sound in the story. An inferred link is raised in the note, not written as fact.

**The piece is ready to send.** When the piece itself was asked for, write all of it, in the voice of whoever signs and addressed to its reader. "Write the exec summary" asks for the text, not an outline with notes on what to say. Nothing in it is addressed to the user, and it has no blanks: no "R$ ___", no "[insert cost]", no bracketed placeholder other than a name or a signature. When a figure is missing, write the sentence so that it stands without the figure, and say in the note what number would strengthen it and where it would go.

**Notes to the user stay outside.** Corrections to the brief, the claim you did not make, assumptions, gaps and options for a stronger version go in a short note before or after the piece, clearly separated, so the piece can be copied as it is.

**Length follows the format.** An executive summary fits on a page; a note to a landlord is a few lines.

## What to deliver

For a piece: the finished text, then the note to the user if there is anything to say.

For a storyline: the central idea in one sentence and the sequence of sections or slides, each with its heading and what goes in it, so that the headings read in order tell the story. If the user asks for slides or a formatted file, use the skills the environment has, with the storyline as content.

Follow the user's heading and format preferences when they have any: some want the message as the heading, others a neutral label with the message in the body. Do not explain the structure used.
